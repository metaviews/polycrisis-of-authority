# Catastrophic Risk

Catastrophic risk in the AI corpus refers to existential and societal-scale risks from advanced AI systems. The term is used in three distinct senses: (1) "existential risk" specifically — risks that would lead to human extinction or permanent curtailment of human potential; (2) "civilizational-scale risks" — risks that would substantially damage the institutions, economies, or governance structures that contemporary humans depend on, without necessarily ending humanity; and (3) "systemic risks" in the regulatory sense — risks of a class that, if realized, would have system-wide effects, as the EU AI Act defines for general-purpose AI models.

The empirical question of how plausible these risks are is genuinely uncertain. The arguments for substantial catastrophic risk include trajectory projections (capability advancing rapidly without corresponding alignment maturation); risk-modeling work (Stuart Russell's concerns about principal-agent failures in advanced AI; Joe Carlsmith's exhaustive analysis of existential risk scenarios); empirical observations about model behaviors (scheming, deception, jailbreaking); and historical precedents of large-scale accidents with new technologies. The arguments against substantial catastrophic risk include the absence of evidence so far for any near-term capability for autonomous catastrophic harm, the substantial self-correction capacity in AI development, the empirical record of prior technology cycles producing large effects without existential risk, and methodological concerns about "pre-mortem" risk modeling.

In the Polycrisis corpus, this concept is the principal reference for simulation frames involving extreme scenarios. Crisis frames that involve severe AI failure (a model behaves catastrophically against its operator; a deployed system causes large-scale harm) can be grounded against this concept. Advisor voices framed as catastrophic-risk researchers, AI-safety coordinators, and existential-risk foundations can be grounded here.

The policy question of how to act under uncertainty about catastrophic outcomes is the substantive challenge. The expected-value arguments vary substantially depending on how one weighs extreme-tail outcomes; the institutional response (focused risk-management, scenario planning, pre-deployment evaluation) is conditioned on a particular theory of how catastrophic risk materializes. The simulation can be used to explore which scenarios produce which institutional responses under different theories of catastrophic risk.
