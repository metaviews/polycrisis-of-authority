# Regulatory Capture

Regulatory capture is the structural condition in which a regulator comes to serve the regulated industry rather than the public interest. The concept has substantial prior tradition — George Stigler's 1971 "Theory of Economic Regulation," the capture literature in political science, the empirical evidence from financial-regulator capture by the industries they oversee. The application to AI regulation is a recent extension.

The substantive AI-capture question is structurally live. The AI Safety Institute model (the UK AISI, the US AISI created in 2024) is by design staffed partly from frontier labs, with the rationale that the evaluation work requires deep technical expertise. This creates a soft-capture risk — evaluators who rotate to industry, or who maintain relationships with the labs they evaluate — but is necessary to maintain technical credibility. The voluntary industry-led bodies (Partnership on AI, Frontier Model Forum) are explicitly multi-stakeholder but structurally industry-leaning. The institutional design question of how to maintain rigor while retaining expertise is unresolved.

The substantive design choices that mitigate capture are well-understood from prior regulation: rotating assignments; clear conflict-of-interest rules; transparency about meetings; structural separation of evaluation from advice; external review by independent academics and civil-society researchers. The AI-AISI model has adopted several of these; the long-term question is whether the institutional design holds over time.

In the Polycrisis corpus, this concept is the substantive reference for simulation frames involving regulator credibility. Crisis frames that invoke "the regulator is captured by industry" or "the regulator is independent and rigorous" can be grounded against this concept. Advisor voices framed as civil-society researchers, accountability advocates, and former regulators can be grounded here.

The simulation can be used to explore capture scenarios under different regulator designs: rotating vs. permanent staff; conflict-of-interest rules of varying strength; transparency regimes; external review presence. The substantive question is which designs produce which outcomes.
