# Chinese Frontier

The "Chinese frontier" in the AI policy corpus refers to the structural concept of the Chinese AI capability frontier — the family of Chinese-frontier labs (DeepSeek, Alibaba/Qwen, Zhipu, Moonshot AI) collectively producing a capability range that is distinct from the US-anchored frontier cohort (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind). The concept is structural rather than literal: it identifies a frontier-capability position held by Chinese-jurisdiction actors, with the policy implications that follow.

The empirical evidence through 2024-2026 is substantive. DeepSeek-V3 (January 2025) demonstrated that the Chinese frontier could produce a comparable model at substantially lower training cost than the US-anchored baseline. Alibaba's Qwen family (Qwen-VL, Qwen-Coder, Qwen-Chat) provides a parallel capability across vision, code, and chat modalities. Zhipu's GLM-4 line and CogVideoX for text-to-video are the principal competing Chinese outputs. Moonshot's Kimi family introduced long-context capability as a Chinese-frontier distinguishing feature.

The substantive question is what the Chinese frontier's existence implies for state-AI competition. Before 2025, the export-control thesis posited that Chinese frontier capability would be substantially limited by US restrictions on advanced compute. The DeepSeek release and subsequent Chinese-frontier outputs provided evidence that the compute gap was narrower than the export-control thesis assumed. The concept now serves as the principal reference for state-competition framing in the AI policy corpus — Crisis frames involving Chinese-frontier labs and their outputs are grounded here.

The Chinese-frontier concept is also relevant for the AI procurement question. Procurement policies in the US, EU, and UK increasingly specify whether Chinese-frontier models are eligible for government use, with substantial implications for the multi-jurisdiction AI deployment picture.
