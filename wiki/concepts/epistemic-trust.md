# Epistemic Trust

Epistemic trust refers to the institutional mechanisms by which societies collectively determine which knowledge claims to trust. The concept is structural — it applies to all knowledge claims, not just AI — but the 2023-2026 era has put unusual pressure on these mechanisms as synthetic media production becomes cheap.

The prior mechanisms for epistemic trust include: professional credentialing (medicine, law, journalism); institutional review (peer review, regulatory approval); reputation systems (academic citation, journalistic reputation); verification infrastructure (fact-checking, debunking); and the informal "epistemic ethos" of a profession (which values which kinds of evidence). These mechanisms work at varying scales and under varying conditions. The structural pressure from synthetic media is real but uneven — different mechanisms are differently vulnerable.

The substantive question is whether existing mechanisms can adapt or whether they need to be replaced. Credentialing is robust to synthetic media (a credentialed physician is still a credentialed physician regardless of the synthetic content they navigate). Peer review is somewhat less robust (if the literature itself is full of synthetic text, peer review has to be skeptical). Fact-checking is most directly under pressure (the cost of producing false content has dropped dramatically). Reputation is mixed (reputation propagates on its own graph, independent of content production).
