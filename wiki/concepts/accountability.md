# Accountability

Accountability in the AI corpus refers to the structural concept of substantive (not procedural) accountability for AI systems — who is answerable when an AI deployment causes harm, the institutional infrastructure that makes accountability real, and the gap between procedural and substantive accountability.

The procedural-vs-substantive distinction is central to the accountability discourse. Procedural accountability is the formal setting: documentation, decision logs, governance committees, regulatory frameworks. Substantive accountability is the actual answerability: when an AI system causes harm, does an accountable party have the information, the authority, and the willingness to act? The 2020s AI accountability discourse has been largely about closing the gap between procedural and substantive accountability — the recognition that existing procedural structures (regulatory filings, model cards, etc.) often produce no substantive answerability.

The civil-society accountability infrastructure (AI Now, AJL, FLI, Partnership on AI's research outputs) is the principal operational instantiation of substantive accountability. Each organization develops substantive analyses of specific AI deployments, constructs accountability cases, and publishes them publicly. The corpus treats these accounts as the principal reference for substantive accountability.

The policy dimension: EU AI Act provisions on accountability, the US executive orders on AI accountability, OECD AI Principles' accountability provisions are all attempts to translate substantive accountability into regulatory structure. The outcomes have been partial — accountability work produces durable analyses but the regulatory frameworks can lag.
