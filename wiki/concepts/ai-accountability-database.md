# AI Accountability Database

The AI Accountability Database is AI Now Institute's structured index of AI accountability actions — incidents, regulatory responses, civil-society cases, federal agency guidance, and academic analyses — that the Institute maintains as a public resource. The Database is one of the principal operational artifacts of the AI accountability discourse; it sits as the most concrete realization of the substantive-versus-procedural accountability gap that the AI Now Institute argues against.

## What the Database contains

The Database's content covers four principal categories:

- **Incidents of AI-caused harm** — concrete cases where AI deployment produced substantive effects on individuals or communities. Examples include Amazon's Ring partnerships with police departments, the various facial-recognition deployment cases documented by AJL and others, and the labor conditions of training-data annotators in some jurisdictions.

- **Civil-society accountability work** — investigations, lawsuits, and civil-society responses to AI deployment. Examples include civil-rights lawsuits against AI-driven employment tools, AJL's "Gender Shades" follow-up work, and the various EPIC and EFF filings on transparency and accountability.

- **Regulatory responses** — federal, state, and local regulatory actions in major jurisdictions. Examples include the EU AI Office's first systemic-risk designations, the US federal executive orders on AI (14110 and successor directives), state-level biometric-AI laws, and city-level restrictions on specific AI deployments.

- **Academic analyses of AI accountability cases** — peer-reviewed studies of specific AI deployments, including bias studies, deployment-impact studies, and accountability-gap analyses. Examples include the algorithmic hiring studies, criminal-justice risk-assessment studies, and healthcare-resource-allocation studies.

The entries are categorized by incident type (algorithmic bias, environmental harm, labor harm, content moderation, etc.), by domain (employment, housing, healthcare, criminal justice, education, etc.), and by jurisdiction (US, EU, UK, China, etc.). The Database is updated regularly as new incidents and responses emerge.

## Why the Database matters

The Database is one of the principal operational artifacts of substantive accountability in the AI corpus. Where procedural accountability produces documentation, decision logs, and regulatory filings without necessarily yielding answerability for AI-caused harm, the Database translates specific cases into specific accountability claims. When the simulation needs to ground an advisor voice or crisis frame in a specific AI-incident case, the Database is one of the primary references.

The Database also provides a methodology of case-construction that other actors can adopt. Civil-society organizations have used the Database's structure as the basis for their own incident-tracking work; regulatory bodies have referenced it; journalists have cited it. The Database is therefore both content (specific cases) and method (case-construction practice).

The empirical record shows growth in case volume through 2024-2026 — the rate of new AI accountability cases has accelerated, particularly around generative-AI deployment (synthetic CSAM, deepfake election content, AI-generated CSAM, and similar). The Database has expanded to cover generative-AI-specific cases alongside the prior computer-vision and algorithmic-decision-making cases.

## In the Polycrisis corpus

In the Polycrisis corpus, the Database is one of the principal operational artifacts of substantive accountability. Crisis frames involving AI incidents, accountability cases, and civil-society response can reference specific Database entries as evidence. The Database is one of the structural anchors of the civil-society-accountability-infrastructure theme — alongside AI Now's annual reports, FLI's policy briefs, and Partnership on AI's research publications.

The concept is structurally analogous to the EPA's EJScreen (environmental justice screening tool), the CFPB's consumer-complaint database, or OSHA's accident-reporting infrastructure — each provides a structured public record that translates scattered incidents into coherent accountability cases. The AI corpus's analog is younger, evolving in real-time, and substantially dependent on civil-society work rather than regulatory mandate.
