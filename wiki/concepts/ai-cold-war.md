# AI Cold War

AI Cold War in the AI corpus refers to the structural concept of geopolitical competition over AI capability framed as a Cold-War-style conflict. The framing originated in 2017-2018 commentary and has evolved substantially through 2024-2026 in response to the DeepSeek-V3 January 2025 release and subsequent Chinese-frontier outputs.

The Cold-War framing rests on several structural analogies: (1) **strategic competition**: state-level competition for capability, with associated concerns about capability gaps; (2) **bifurcation**: technology-stack bifurcation into competing jurisdictions, with implications for global supply chains; (3) **alliance structure**: allied-jurisdiction coordination, including export controls and joint evaluations; (4) **ideological component**: AI as a domain of ideological competition in addition to strategic competition.

The empirical evolution of the framing through 2025-2026 is substantial. The DeepSeek-V3 release in January 2025 provided evidence that the export-control thesis (compute restrictions would substantially delay Chinese frontier) had been less effective than expected, producing the canonical "Sputnik moment" framing. Subsequent Chinese-frontier outputs (Qwen, Zhipu, Moonshot) sustained the trajectory.

The substantive question of whether the "AI Cold War" framing remains accurate post-DeepSeek is contested. Optimists argue that the cost-efficiency shift means frontier capability is more distributed, undermining the Cold-War framing. Pessimists argue that the bifurcation of compute, data, and talent is structural, sustaining the Cold-War frame. The empirical record supports both interpretations.

In the Polycrisis corpus, this concept is the principal reference for the geopolitical-comparison dimension of AI policy.
