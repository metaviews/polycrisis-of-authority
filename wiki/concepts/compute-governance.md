# Compute Governance

Compute governance refers to regulation through access to compute capacity: export controls on advanced chips, GPU licensing for frontier training, reporting requirements for hyperscaler capacity, and the substantive policy effects of intervening at the substrate level. The concept is structural — most AI policy effects flow through compute access; even regulations that target AI directly depend on the underlying compute substrate.

The substantive content falls into three dimensions. **Export controls**: the US BIS rules (October 2022, expanded October 2023) restrict advanced GPU shipments to specific jurisdictions; the EU has not implemented equivalent restrictions; allied jurisdictions (UK, Japan) align with US rules. **Licensing**: frontier-training compute licensing — China uses pre-training licensing for large-scale AI training, requires services-registration, and reviews cap-deployment locations. **Reporting**: the US executive order 14110 (and successor directives) requires disclosure of substantial compute acquisitions; the EU AI Act requires disclosure of compute used to train systemic-risk models.

The empirical question of whether export controls effectively delay frontier capability has been contested since 2022. The DeepSeek-V3 release of January 2025 — produced at substantially lower training cost than the export-control thesis assumed — was the principal evidence in this debate. The interpretive question is whether the controls meaningfully slowed Chinese frontier development, had no effect, or compounded chinese capability through forced efficiency innovations. Each interpretation has advocates.
