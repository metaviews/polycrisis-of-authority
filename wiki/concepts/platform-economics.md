# Platform Economics

Platform economics in the AI corpus refers to the structural question of where AI value is captured, the role of network effects in AI industries, and the dynamics that determine durable moats. The concept connects prior platform-economics work (Rochet and Tirole's two-sided markets; Parker and Van Alstyne's platform economics) to AI-specific dynamics.

The platform-economic question for AI has several distinct structural elements. **Distribution moat**: a frontier lab with API access to many customers has a distribution advantage; the customer's lock-in compounds over time. **Data moat**: each user interaction produces training signal that improves the model; the data advantage compounds. **Compute moat**: a frontier lab with substantial compute has an inherent scale advantage; smaller labs cannot match the training cost. **Talent moat**: a frontier lab with senior researchers produces novel advances; the talent advantage compounds. The four moats interact — compute enables talent, which produces data, which produces distribution.

The empirical record through 2025-2026 is mixed. The frontier-lab structure suggests that one or two labs will continue to hold the frontier-c capability position; the historical pattern of platforms (search: Google; mobile: Apple/iOS; social: Meta) supports this. The countervailing evidence is that AI has higher training-cost trajectories than prior platform waves, slowing the platform-leadership race; and that open-weight alternatives (Meta's Llama, DeepSeek, Mistral, Qwen) provide non-frontier alternatives with substantial capability.
