# Labor Displacement and Class

The question of how AI displaces labor has shifted through 2023-2025 from speculative ("what will AI do to jobs?") to empirical ("where is the displacement landing, and what are the political effects?"). The current state of the corpus is grounded in three observations: first, the *exposure* estimates — Acemoglu's estimates that AI exposure affects roughly half of US tasks but with substitution elasticities that produce a 0.5% productivity effect, MIT/IBM research on the same; second, the *deployment* evidence — Klarna's customer-service substitution, McDonald's drive-through experiments, the early evidence on which work is actually being replaced; third, the *political* effects — the labor coalition politics of AI displacement, the question of who benefits from productivity gains, the relationship between AI displacement and broader labor-market shifts.

The displacement is *uneven*. Higher-skill work that has a clear automation trajectory (initial customer-service triage, some legal document review, some radiologic image assessment) sees direct substitution. Higher-skill work that requires judgment under uncertainty (most of medicine, much of law, much of strategic decision-making) sees AI as augmentation rather than replacement, but with significant productivity effects on the worker using it. Lower-skill work varies — physical labor is largely unaffected in the short term; clerical work is heavily exposed; customer-facing service is partially exposed and partially automation-complementary.

The political-economy consequence is that the AI labor question doesn't fit a single class story. Higher-skill workers benefit from AI augmentation (productivity gains, wage pressure on the lowest-tier of their profession). Mid-skill workers see the largest substitution effects (customer-service, clerical, mid-tier of professional services). Low-skill workers are partially insulated in the short term (physical labor) but face longer-term substitution in retail, food service, and logistics as robotics and AI converge.

The class-formation question is less settled. The displacement profile — concentrated in mid-skill clerical work, partial in higher-skill professional work, deferred in lower-skill physical work — doesn't map cleanly onto the historical political coalitions that formed around past technology transitions (agrarian populism, manufacturing unionism, professional-class credentialism). The simulation can be used to explore which coalitions form under which displacement scenarios.
