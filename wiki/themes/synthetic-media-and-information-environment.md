# Synthetic Media and Information Environment

The 2023-2026 generative AI revolution produced a structural shift in the cost of producing realistic synthetic media — text, images, audio, video. The implication for the information environment is structural: the cost of producing persuasive false content dropped by orders of magnitude; the cost of detecting synthetic content is rising. The policy question of how to maintain trust in a media environment in which synthetic content is abundant has become one of the central questions of the AI policy conversation.

The structural challenge is not solely one of deepfakes. The genuine 2016 disinformation problem was already about content distribution, not content production; the AI revolution extends the same structural pattern with much better production tools. The 2024 election cycles (US, EU, India, others) all had synthetic-media components — some real (deepfaked calls to voters, generated images in disinformation campaigns) and some performative (candidates and media outlets highlighting "look how good the deepfakes are now" without significant operational effect).

The policy response has been heterogeneous. The C2PA coalition (Adobe, Microsoft, Google, OpenAI, and others) has developed a content-provenance standard using cryptographic signatures on media; the standard is technically credible but has limited voluntary adoption. The US federal government's executive order on AI (14110) included content-authenticity requirements, but the implementation has been limited. The EU's AI Act includes transparency requirements for synthetic media. The substantive policy question — what to require of generative-AI providers around content provenance — remains unsettled.

The structural question of *cognitive authority* is the deeper version of this theme. The "locus of political legitimacy" (per the cognitive-authority concept) shifts when the cost of producing realistic synthetic content drops, because the institutional response to political claims (verification, expert evaluation, trust in published sources) becomes harder. The simulation can model the cognitive-authority question through this theme, particularly the question of how a player's policy interventions affect the trust institutions in the simulated regime.
