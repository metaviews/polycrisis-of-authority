# AI in Procurement and State Power

Government procurement is the largest underutilized lever in the AI policy conversation. The United States federal government alone spends roughly $700B annually on procurement; even a small percentage of that directed toward AI evaluation, deployment, and standard-setting creates substantial private-sector incentives. The White House's M-24-10 memorandum (March 2024) on "Advancing Governance, Innovation, and Risk Management in Agency Use of Artificial Intelligence" required federal agencies to advance their AI use and governance; the accompanying OMB guidance required designated agency AI leads and risk-management practices for AI deployments.

The procurement lever is structurally different from regulation. Regulation imposes requirements on systems that are deployed; procurement shapes the systems that are developed in the first place by creating demand for specific capabilities (evaluation, transparency, auditability, specific safety properties). A federal AI safety institute's procurement of frontier-model evaluations is what gives the institute leverage over frontier labs; a state-procurement requirement that vendors publish a model card is what gives the model-card ecosystem real adoption.

The allied-procurement dimension is the underappreciated strategic angle. UK, US, EU, and allied-jurisdiction procurement offices can coordinate on shared AI standards (the OECD AI Policy Observatory, the G7 Hiroshima AI Process), and that coordination can be a non-tariff trade instrument. A "trusted-AI vendor" list, jointly curated by allied procurement offices, can give the allied states leverage over both domestic and foreign AI vendors that wouldn't be available through individual-jurisdiction regulation.
