# State-AI Strategic Competition

The narrative of "state-AI strategic competition" in 2025-2026 refers to the structural competition between (and among) major state-jurisdictions over AI capability, supply-chain access, and the policy alignment that follows. The narrative reframes considerably with the January 2025 DeepSeek release, which provided evidence that the United States' export-control regime (restricting advanced GPU shipments to China) had not produced the structural delay its proponents assumed.

The competition is multi-dimensional. First, the **capability** dimension: which state's frontier labs and research institutions can ship comparable models. DeepSeek-V3 (January 2025) and subsequent chinese frontier outputs (Qwen, Zhipu, Moonshot) extended the chinese frontier in capability terms; the prior expectation that US export controls would prevent a comparable Chinese frontier by 2025-2026 was falsified within the 2024-2025 window. Second, the **supply chain** dimension: access to advanced compute (TSMC fabrication, NVIDIA H100 / B200, energy for datacenter), the structural dependencies on Taiwanese semiconductor fabrication, and the policy interventions (US export controls, EU Chips Act, Chinese industrial policy) that shape who has what. Third, the **alignment** dimension: whose policy regime governs the deployment of frontier AI, and the cross-jurisdiction implications of foundation-model availability (the EU AI Act's extraterritorial provisions, US export controls, Chinese services-registration requirements).

The Metaviews archive frames this as a "geopolitical" question rather than a "trade" question. The reframing matters: a "trade" framing treats the state-ai competition as a balance-of-payments issue (export controls are a tariff-equivalent); a "geopolitical" framing treats it as a long-run strategic posture (compute, data, talent, and policy levers are elements of a national-security posture that compounds). The DeepSeek moment specifically shifted the framing: even those who previously argued for trade-style export controls as a tariff-equivalent had to re-evaluate when the chinese frontier produced at substantially lower training cost than the export-control thesis assumed.
