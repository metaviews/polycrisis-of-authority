# NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) is the US Department of Commerce's standards laboratory, with statutory authority under the National Institute of Standards and Technology Act to develop technical standards and guidance across federal use. The **AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)**, released in January 2023, is the US federal government's primary voluntary artifact on AI governance. It defines four function categories (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and is structured around trustworthiness characteristics including valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed.

The companion **Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1)**, released in July 2024, addresses the specific risks of generative AI models — confabulation, dangerous information, data privacy, intellectual property, etc. Together, AI RMF 1.0 and the GenAI Profile form the US government's principal point of reference when procurement officials, regulators, and corporate compliance teams ask "what does good AI governance look like."

In the Polycrisis corpus, NIST's AI RMF is significant because the framework's voluntary nature positions the US federal approach differently from the EU's AI Act (binding) and the UK's AISI evaluations (operational). The corpus treats NIST as the durable foundation, the EU as the binding regulatory layer, and the AISI as the operational practice layer — three complementary instruments, three different jurisdictions, three different policy theories. Simulations in Polycrisis reference NIST when they need a "mainstream US federal" framing without taking a binding-regulatory stance.

## In the Polycrisis corpus

Metaviews coverage of NIST and its AI artifacts focuses on (1) the framework structure as a usable artifact for procurement and compliance teams; (2) the international coordination angle (NIST participates in the OECD AI Policy Observatory, the G7 Hiroshima AI Process, and the joint evaluations with the UK AISI); (3) the boundary between voluntary framework and binding regulation (executive orders, sector-specific rules), where NIST's role is to provide a shared vocabulary that other bodies can refer to. The corpus treats NIST as the institution that operationalizes the abstract: it does not promulgate binding law (that's Congress, regulatory agencies, and the courts), but it provides the technical infrastructure that any actual enforcement cannot do without.
